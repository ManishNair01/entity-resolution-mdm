# Extreme Ages Signal Review, Not Fraud

**People aged 100 or older are exceptionally uncommon: Australia estimated 6,181 centenarians in 2022, equal to 238 per million residents, or 0.0238% of the population.** The project’s publishable count of records with valid dates of birth that imply age 100+, its record-level rate, and its comparison with Australian prevalence are **[TBD]** until the pipeline produces them in `reports/metrics.json`. Official prevalence nevertheless supports using age 100+ as a neutral enhanced-review trigger and investigating the dataset’s reference date and synthetic DOB-generation process. It does not show that any individual is deceased, fraudulent, engaged in identity theft, or represented by a stale record. The dataset is synthetic and contains duplicates, so a future pipeline-produced record rate must be interpreted as a data-quality indicator rather than as population prevalence. Extreme age should remain an auditable review flag until independent evidence establishes a more specific outcome.

## Australian centenarians remain roughly one in 4,200

The most relevant demographic benchmark is Australia because Febrl 3 models Australian customer data. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare reports that the population aged 100 or older increased from 226 people in 1973 to **6,181 people in 2022**, while prevalence rose from 16 to **238 centenarians per million residents**. That 2022 rate equals 23.8 per 100,000, 0.0238%, or about one person in 4,202; AIHW explicitly describes centenarians as a very small part of the population ([AIHW, *How long can Australians live?*](https://www.aihw.gov.au/getmedia/da6b92c7-60c2-4c66-aab7-63bb26fc71fd/aihw-phe-324.pdf.aspx?inline=true); [AIHW summary](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/life-expectancy-deaths/how-long-can-australians-live/summary)). Australia’s 2021 Census produced a similar order of magnitude: 5,547 people aged 100+ among 25,422,788 usual residents, approximately **21.82 per 100,000** ([ABS age-distribution review](https://www.abs.gov.au/census/about-census/census-statistical-independent-assurance-panel-report/34-population-counts-and-age-sex-distributions); [ABS 2021 population](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/population/population-census/2021)).

The rarity is not uniquely Australian. Recent official figures place the UK at **24.0 centenarians per 100,000** in 2024, the United States at **24.2 per 100,000** in the 2020 Census, and Japan—an unusually old population—at **63.7 per 100,000** in 2023 ([UK ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/ageing/bulletins/estimatesoftheveryoldincludingcentenarians/latest); [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/2020/c2020sr-02.pdf)). These comparisons do not require a customer dataset to mirror a national population. They establish that centenarians remain rare even in an unusually old population and provide an external reasonableness benchmark for the project metric once the pipeline produces it.

A DOB can be a valid calendar date while yielding a misleading present-day age if the synthetic data was generated for an earlier period, if birth years were sampled without age constraints, or if the intended reference year was never specified. **Calculating age long after the dataset’s intended reference period can move many otherwise valid birth dates across the 100+ threshold.** The demographic evidence therefore makes a reference-date or generation artifact a strong dataset-level hypothesis if the pipeline confirms an anomalous rate. Population statistics cannot reveal which DOBs are correct, and the project should verify the Febrl documentation or generation logic before asserting a cause.

## Extreme-age data deserves corroboration, not automatic rejection

Authoritative agencies themselves treat extreme ages as unusually sensitive to data error. AIHW notes that a birth year entered as 1875 instead of 1895 adds 20 years to an apparent lifespan and that Australian ages at death do not receive the same rigorous validation as specialist longevity databases ([AIHW, pp. 24–25](https://www.aihw.gov.au/getmedia/da6b92c7-60c2-4c66-aab7-63bb26fc71fd/aihw-phe-324.pdf.aspx?inline=true)). The U.S. Census Bureau identifies missing-age allocation, deliberate exaggeration, question design, processing, and privacy procedures as sources of distortion in centenarian counts. In one historical review, only 271 of 585 sampled records initially classified as centenarians were confirmed from original forms ([U.S. Census Bureau, *Centenarians: 2020*](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/2020/c2020sr-02.pdf)). The UK ONS validates very old ages by matching birth and death records using several attributes, including date and place of birth, full name, and aliases ([ONS high-age evaluation](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/ageing/methodologies/accuracyofofficialhighagepopulationestimatesinenglandandwalesanevaluation)).

Those practices support stronger verification, but they also show why a valid centenarian DOB must not be discarded merely for being rare. The appropriate question is whether independent, authoritative sources corroborate the claimed birth details. For identity verification generally, UK government guidance separates checking whether a person is alive from checking whether an identity was stolen or is synthetic, and higher-confidence fraud checks draw on independent authoritative sources ([GOV.UK identity verification guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/identity-proofing-and-verification-of-an-individual/how-to-prove-and-verify-someones-identity)). The current synthetic dataset does not contain birth records, death registrations, verified activity, documents, biometrics, or investigation outcomes, so it can identify uncertainty but cannot resolve it.

## Age alone cannot establish death, fraud, or staleness

Death, fraud, identity theft, and staleness are separate hypotheses requiring different evidence. U.S. Social Security guidance accepts evidence such as a death certificate, certified public death record, physician or funeral-director statement, coroner report, or specified official government report; age alone is not evidence of death ([SSA Handbook §1720](https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/handbook/handbook.17/handbook-1720.html); [SSA preferred evidence](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0200304005)). The SSA Inspector General described very-old records without death information as *very likely* deceased only after considering additional facts such as absent earnings and benefit payments, and it separately cross-checked employment systems to identify potential identifier misuse ([SSA OIG testimony](https://oig.ssa.gov/congressional-testimony/2015-03-17-newsroom-congressional-testimony-march16-hsgac/)). SSA also documents cases in which living people were mistakenly recorded as dead, reinforcing the danger of assigning a life-status outcome from a proxy ([SSA erroneous-death procedure](https://secure.ssa.gov/apps10/poms.nsf/links/0203316095)).

Staleness is equally distinct: it concerns when a record was last verified or used, not how old the customer is. A 104-year-old customer record with recent verified activity can be current; a 40-year-old customer record untouched for years can be stale. Identity misuse would require contradictory activity or authoritative counter-fraud evidence, while a confirmed death would require accepted life-status evidence. The age flag should therefore record only what the data establishes: the DOB implies an extreme age under a stated calculation date and needs enhanced review.

## The defensible policy preserves evidence and uncertainty

For this portfolio project, the data-quality policy should state: **“A calendar-valid DOB implying age 100 or older triggers enhanced review. The source DOB is preserved, and the age flag does not by itself invalidate the DOB, exclude the record from entity resolution, or classify the customer as deceased, stale, fraudulent, or a victim of identity theft.”** The review record should contain a stable record key, calculated age, fixed calculation date, neutral reason such as `age_100_plus`, review status, evidence source or assurance level, and review timestamp. Retaining verification evidence or its assurance level aligns with government guidance that electronic identity checking should use multiple sources or a suitably governed authoritative source and preserve the basis for its conclusion ([HMRC electronic verification guidance](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/economic-crime-supervision-handbook/ecsh33357)).

Within the present dataset, the appropriate status for any age-flagged rows is unresolved pending review. A production workflow could later distinguish `age_verified`, `dob_corrected_from_authoritative_source`, `deceased_confirmed`, `potential_identity_misuse_referred`, and `unable_to_verify`, but the current project cannot populate those outcomes from the supplied fields. ## Conclusion

The owner’s intuition is supported: people aged 100+ are rare enough that records crossing this threshold warrant enhanced review. The size of the project’s concentration and its difference from Australian prevalence remain **[TBD]** until produced by the pipeline. If the resulting concentration is materially above the external benchmark, an unstated reference date or synthetic-generation effect should be investigated before interpreting it as widespread death-record failure or identity fraud.

The clean design is to preserve three layers of meaning: a DOB can be calendar-valid, its derived age can trigger review, and a later evidence-based process can determine disposition. Keeping those layers separate produces a rule that is defensible in an interview, auditable in a pipeline, and fair to the small number of real centenarians whom an automatic invalidation rule would misclassify.
